Emergency management jobs in the GTA public sector are planning and coordination jobs. The people in them write emergency plans, run practice exercises, train staff and help lead the response when something goes wrong. They are not firefighters or paramedics. The work exists in every Ontario municipality and provincial ministry, because Ontario law requires each of them to run an emergency management program.

This guide explains who hires, what the jobs ask for, what November 2026 postings paid and how to build the skills. The rules come from Ontario's and Toronto's own pages, checked on November 18, 2026. The job examples come from postings on PublicJobs.ca on November 17, 2026.

Key takeaways

  • Ontario's Emergency Management and Civil Protection Act requires every municipality and provincial ministry to have an emergency management program.
  • Every municipality must name an emergency management program coordinator and an emergency information officer.
  • Public employers that are not ministries or city offices hire for this work too. On November 17, 2026, the IESO and Toronto Community Housing had open preparedness roles.
  • Pay in three postings open on November 17, 2026: $1,596.46 to $1,950.31 a week for a provincial Field Officer, $118,456 to $169,208 a year at the IESO, and $123,900.46 to $136,290.51 a year at Toronto Community Housing.
  • Ontario's Incident Management System (IMS) training is free online, in English and French.

What emergency management work is

Ontario describes emergency management in five parts, which it calls pillars. Its municipal councillor's guide lists them:

Pillar What it means Example from Ontario
Prevention Stop the emergency from happening Keeping new buildings away from flood plains
Mitigation Reduce the damage if it happens Rebuilding a road with larger drain pipes (culverts)
Preparedness Get ready before it happens Having a municipal emergency response plan
Response Act during the emergency Giving shelter to residents
Recovery Rebuild after it Rebuilding damaged infrastructure

In the three postings later in this guide, most listed duties are prevention and preparedness work: inspections, plans, exercises and training. All three ads also include some work during emergencies or incidents.

Why every GTA municipality needs this work

Ontario's emergency management program resources page says all municipalities and provincial ministries must have an emergency management program under the Emergency Management and Civil Protection Act. The program has four parts:

  • an emergency plan,
  • training programs and exercises,
  • public education on emergency preparedness, and
  • any other standards set for emergency programs.

The councillor's guide adds the details from the regulation, Ontario Regulation 380/04. Every municipality must keep:

  • an emergency management program committee,
  • an emergency control group to coordinate its response in an emergency,
  • an emergency operations centre, and
  • an emergency response plan.

Each municipality must also name staff, or a member of council, as its emergency management program coordinator and its emergency information officer. The guide says the coordinator is the municipality's main link to the province's emergency operations centre. The municipality must keep its program in line with the Act every year.

The guide adds that having designated and trained staff is critical to responding quickly to emergencies. Toronto has a full office for this work, called Toronto Emergency Management.

Who hires emergency managers in the GTA

This guide covers three kinds of public employer.

Municipalities

Toronto's Emergency Plan (September 2024 edition, linked from the City's emergency plan page) shows how big the work gets in a large city. Toronto Emergency Management:

  • runs an on-call service 24 hours a day, 7 days a week,
  • chairs a working group of director-level staff from dozens of City divisions, agencies and corporations, and
  • did the City's hazard identification and risk assessment.

The working group includes Toronto Community Housing, the TTC and Toronto Hydro, so emergency planning reaches well beyond one office. The plan also says staff in the City's emergency operations centre must take required training first. The centre works under the Incident Management System.

A search of job titles on PublicJobs.ca on November 18, 2026 found no open emergency management job at a GTA municipality. Municipal emergency management jobs do open, so it helps to watch for them.

For how the City hires, see our guide to how City of Toronto hiring works.

The province

The councillor's guide calls the Ministry of Emergency Preparedness and Response the overall provincial emergency management coordinator. It says the ministry's Field Officers are stationed throughout Ontario to advise and help communities. The ministry also runs the Provincial Emergency Operations Centre around the clock.

Ontario's careers and professional development page talks about Emergency Management Ontario (EMO). It says EMO looks for team players with proven communication skills, analytical ability and good judgement. Our guide to how the Ontario Public Service hires covers the application and interview steps.

Agencies and public corporations

Agencies that run critical services plan for emergencies too, often under the name business continuity. Business continuity means keeping essential services running during a disruption. The IESO, which runs Ontario's power grid, is one example. Toronto Community Housing, which is owned by the City of Toronto, is another.

Ontario's program resources page lists nine sectors of critical infrastructure in its Critical Infrastructure Assurance Program. They include the electrical power system, transportation networks and continuity of government.

Field Officer, Ministry of Emergency Preparedness and Response

The job ad says you would help communities with emergency planning and design, deliver and evaluate emergency exercises. You would also be part of the provincial emergency response team, ready to deploy on short notice for long periods.

The mandatory requirements in the ad:

  • a valid Class G driver's licence or equivalent, to reach sites that transit may not serve,
  • being able and willing to work irregular and extended hours, especially during emergencies, and to be on call on a regular basis, and
  • being willing and able to travel now and then to other provinces, territories and the United States.

The ad also asks for knowledge of emergency management principles and relevant legislation, and experience using adult learning principles to design and deliver training. It lists 1 permanent job and 2 temporary jobs of up to 6 months, with possible extension.

Senior Specialist Business Continuity, IESO

The Independent Electricity System Operator manages Ontario's power grid in real time. This role leads its Emergency Preparedness and Business Continuity Program. The ad says the work includes training, exercises, business impact assessments (finding which processes matter most and how fast they must recover) and supporting the chair of the Emergency Response Team. Pay runs from step 1 to step 10, for a 35-hour week.

The ad asks for 8 or more years of experience in emergency preparedness, business continuity, operational resilience, risk management, or equivalent work. It asks for a bachelor's degree in a field such as business, engineering, risk management or emergency management, or equivalent experience. A professional certification from the Disaster Recovery Institute is listed as an asset. The ad says the job is unionized and comes with a defined benefit pension plan, which pays a set amount in retirement. It also says the IESO is moving toward working in the office five days a week.

Manager, Fire Prevention and Preparedness, Toronto Community Housing

This role sits in TCHC's Emergency Response and Compliance department. It leads the fire life safety program, and a team of Fire Prevention Inspectors checks that buildings meet the Ontario Fire Code. Duties include tenant and staff fire safety education, fire safety plans and incident investigation. The manager also attends fire incidents and supports the TCHC emergency operations centre as required.

The ad requires a diploma or degree in a related area, such as fire life safety, risk management, emergency management or occupational health and safety. It also requires IMS 100, IMS 200 and BEM 200 certification.

The job is a full-time, permanent, non-union management job with a hybrid schedule of 36.25 hours a week, and a defined benefit pension with OMERS.

Skills and training that help

All three ads ask you to train or educate other people. The Field Officer ad also asks for knowledge of the law and for working under pressure. The IESO ad asks for risk and business continuity knowledge. The TCHC ad asks for Ontario Fire Code knowledge. You can build these skills in three ways.

Free training from Ontario. Ontario's Incident Management System, or IMS, gives responders in Ontario standard structures, processes and terms. The program resources page says IMS training applies to all emergency management responders in Ontario. It is free in English and French on the Emergency Management Ontario Training Portal, which is linked from that page. The TCHC ad requires IMS 100 and IMS 200. It also requires BEM 200, so check the course list on the portal. Ontario also offers an online course called EM 125, Exercise programs: An introduction.

College and university programs. Ontario's careers page lists several programs. It says Emergency Management Ontario does not endorse any one of them. GTA options on that list include:

  • Centennial College's Emergency Management and Public Safety Institute,
  • George Brown College's Emergency Management program,
  • Toronto Metropolitan University's Certificate in Emergency Management, Threat and Response Planning, and
  • York University's undergraduate degree, graduate program and certificate in Disaster and Emergency Management.

Experience that transfers. None of the three ads requires a degree in emergency management. The IESO ad accepts business, engineering or risk management degrees, or equivalent experience. If you work in risk, operations, facilities, safety, IT recovery or communications today, your experience may count. Read each ad's qualifications closely and show how you meet them, one by one.

How to find these jobs

Emergency management jobs do not always say "emergency" in the title. Search for these words too:

  • business continuity,
  • preparedness,
  • resilience,
  • incident management, and
  • emergency planning.

Frequently asked questions

What does an emergency management coordinator do in Ontario?

Each Ontario municipality must name a staff member, or a member of council, as its emergency management program coordinator. The program includes the emergency plan, training, exercises and public education. The councillor's guide says the coordinator is the municipality's main link to the Provincial Emergency Operations Centre, and links to other groups that may help in a response. Ontario's pages do not list daily duties, so read each job ad.

Do I need a degree in emergency management?

No. None of the three jobs in this guide requires a degree in emergency management. The TCHC ad requires a diploma or degree in a related area. The IESO ad accepts degrees in fields such as business or engineering, or equivalent experience. The Field Officer ad lists no degree. Each employer sets its own requirements, so read the job ad.

Is emergency management training free in Ontario?

IMS training is free online in English and French through the Emergency Management Ontario Training Portal. College and university programs usually charge tuition, so check each school's site.

Do these jobs include on-call or after-hours work?

Some do. The Field Officer ad requires irregular and extended hours, especially during emergencies, and regular on-call duty. The TCHC ad lists Monday to Friday, 36.25 hours a week. It also says the manager attends fire incidents and the TCHC emergency operations centre as required.

What is the difference between emergency management and business continuity?

Emergency management covers a community before, during and after an emergency. Business continuity keeps an organization's own essential services running during a disruption. Some jobs, like the IESO role, cover both.
