A government job can be worth a lower salary, but only if you count the whole package. Salary is one part. The others are often a defined benefit pension that your employer pays into, health and dental benefits, paid leave and union coverage. In Canada, public-sector employees earned more per hour on average than private-sector employees in 2024. Many private-sector workers also have no workplace pension. So a lower salary in a public job may not be a real pay cut. This guide shows how to check, with numbers.

The facts come from Statistics Canada, the OMERS pension plan, the Ontario Pension Board and PublicJobs.ca's own job data. All were checked on November 23, 2026. Most of the pay and benefit figures are for all of Canada; the pension plan and job figures are for Ontario. This guide gives general information, not financial advice. For how public hiring works in Toronto, see our guide to how to get a government job in Toronto.

Key takeaways

  • In 2024, public-sector employees in Canada earned $40.83 an hour on average. Private-sector employees earned $33.35. Education and the kind of work explain part of the gap.
  • Private-sector wages grew faster: up 22.0% since 2006, compared with 7.6% in the public sector, after inflation.
  • In Ontario in 2025, public-sector pension plans had about 1.43 million active members and private-sector plans about 1.45 million. The private sector has more than three times as many employees. The numbers come from two different surveys, so this is a rough comparison.
  • OMERS, the plan at the City of Toronto and many other Ontario municipal employers, takes 9.0% of pay up to $74,600 and 14.6% above that in 2026, for a normal retirement age of 65. Your employer pays the same amount again.
  • In 2024, 80.9% of public-sector employees had medical or dental coverage at work, compared with 62.1% in the private sector.
  • On November 20, 2026, 70% of the jobs on PublicJobs.ca posted a salary. The median posted minimum was $79,644.

Do government jobs pay less than private jobs?

Not on average. Statistics Canada's Quality of Employment report (August 2025) says public-sector employees earned $40.83 an hour in 2024. Private-sector employees earned $33.35.

The same report gives three reasons for the gap:

  • Education. 52.8% of public-sector employees had a bachelor's degree or higher, compared with 31.8% in the private sector.
  • Type of work. 27.6% of private-sector employees worked in sales and service jobs, which tend to pay less. Public-sector employees were more likely to work in education, law, and social, community and government services.
  • Unions. 76.6% of public-sector employees were covered by a collective agreement, compared with 15.0% in the private sector.

So the average does not tell you what you would earn. Depending on your field, a public job may pay less, the same or more than you earn now. The only fair test is the pay range on the posting you want, next to your own pay today.

The report also shows the gap getting smaller. After inflation, average private-sector wages rose 22.0% from 2006 to 2024. Public-sector wages rose 7.6%.

Salary is only part of the offer

When people say "pay cut", they usually compare salary only. A fair comparison has five parts:

Part of the offer What to check Where to find it
Base pay The pay range, and where in it you would start The job posting, then ask at the offer stage
Pension Plan type, your contribution rate and the employer's The posting, the offer, the plan's own website
Health and dental What is covered, and when coverage starts The offer letter or benefits booklet
Paid leave Vacation days and sick leave The offer, or the collective agreement for unionized jobs
Job type Permanent, contract, temporary or part-time The posting

The job type matters a lot. Pension and benefit rules can differ for contract or part-time work, so check them for the exact job.

The pension: the part people often leave out

A defined benefit (DB) pension is a plan that pays you a set monthly amount for life after you retire. The amount comes from a formula, not from how your investments did. A defined contribution (DC) plan is different: it gives you a savings account, and your income depends on how much is in it when you retire.

OMERS (the Ontario Municipal Employees Retirement System) is the plan for many Ontario municipal employers. The City of Toronto is one. A City report says $603.6 million went into OMERS for City staff in 2025, split equally between the City and its employees. OMERS says on its new member page and its pension calculation page that:

  • You and your employer contribute equal amounts.
  • Your pension is based on your years in the plan and your best 5 consecutive years of earnings. Its pension calculation page explains the formula.
  • Pensions have inflation protection that increases them each year.

Two plans cover Ontario Public Service jobs. The Public Service Pension Plan (PSPP) is run by the Ontario Pension Board. The OPSEU Pension Plan is run by OPTrust, and the Government of Ontario and OPSEU sponsor it together. Check your offer, or ask the employer, which plan applies. Ontario agencies and Crown corporations may use other plans. The Ontario Pension Board says the PSPP is a contributory DB plan, and that you and your employer both contribute. On average, contributions fund only 20% to 25% of the pension. Investment returns fund the rest. The PSPP pension is indexed to inflation each year.

Federal jobs use a different plan. Our federal hiring guide explains its formula.

What members pay in

The Ontario Pension Board publishes the member rates for several Ontario public plans on the same page:

Plan Rate on pay up to the YMPE Rate on pay above the YMPE
PSPP (from April 1, 2026) 8.7% 12.0%
OPSEU Pension Plan (OPTrust) 9.4% 11.0%
OMERS (2026) 9.0% 14.6%

The YMPE (year's maximum pensionable earnings) is a yearly earnings limit linked to the Canada Pension Plan. OMERS gives it as $74,600 for 2026. OMERS rates change on January 1, 2027.

A worked example

Say you earn $85,000 a year in an OMERS job in 2026, with a normal retirement age of 65:

  • 9.0% of the first $74,600 = $6,714
  • 14.6% of the other $10,400 = $1,518
  • Your total: about $8,232 a year, taken from your pay.

Your employer then pays the same amount, so about $16,464 a year is paid into the plan for your job. In a DB plan this does not build a personal account. Your pension comes from the formula.

This cuts both ways. Your take-home pay will be lower than the salary suggests. But you are building a lifetime, inflation-protected pension that many private-sector workers don't have.

How many workers have a workplace pension?

Statistics Canada counts active members of registered pension plans in Ontario, by whether the plan is a public-sector or private-sector plan (table 11-10-0133). It counts employees by sector in a different survey (table 14-10-0027). For Ontario in 2025:

Sector Employees Active members of that sector's pension plans
Public sector about 1.62 million about 1.43 million
Private sector about 5.50 million about 1.45 million

The two tables come from different surveys, and the plan count does not include group RRSPs or other workplace savings plans. So treat this as a rough guide. Still, it suggests most public-sector workers have a workplace pension plan. In the private sector, it is closer to 1 in 4.

Benefits, leave and retirement age

Statistics Canada's summer 2025 update on job quality found these gaps for Canada in 2024:

Benefit Public sector Private sector
Medical or dental coverage at work 80.9% 62.1%
Paid vacation leave 81.7% 70.0%

Statistics Canada says the higher union rate may have helped public-sector workers get these benefits.

In 2025, public-sector employees retired earlier. Statistics Canada's retirement age table puts the median retirement age in 2025 at 61.8 for public-sector employees and 65.4 for private-sector employees. That is across Canada, for people who retired that year.

A checklist before you take a lower salary

Use these questions to decide if a public job is worth less salary for you:

  1. What is the real salary gap? Compare the posted range with your current base pay, not your pay with bonuses you may not get again.
  2. What pension do you have now? If your current employer has no pension or a small match, a DB pension adds a lot. If you already have a good pension, the gain is smaller.
  3. What will you pay into the pension? Work out your contribution, as in the example above. Then add the employer's equal share.
  4. What do benefits cost you now? If you pay for your own health or dental plan, a public job may save you that cost.
  5. Is the job permanent? Contract and temporary jobs can have different pension and benefit rules. Check before you accept.
  6. How will your pay grow? Ask whether the job has a pay scale with set steps, and how you move up it.
  7. What else matters to you? Union coverage, hours and retiring earlier all have value, but only you can price them.

A public job's pension and benefits add the most when your current job has little of either. If the salary gap is large and you already have a strong pension, the public job's extras may not make up the gap.

Frequently asked questions

Do government jobs in Canada pay more than private jobs?

On average, yes. Statistics Canada found public-sector employees earned $40.83 an hour in 2024, against $33.35 in the private sector. But higher education levels and the type of work explain part of the gap. Your own result depends on your field.

Does my employer match my pension contributions?

In OMERS, yes. OMERS says you and your employer contribute equal amounts. In the PSPP, you and your employer both contribute. Check the plan's own website for the plan named in your offer.

Is a government pension protected from inflation?

OMERS says its inflation protection increases pensions each year. The Ontario Pension Board says PSPP pensions are indexed to inflation each year. Rules can differ by plan, so check yours.

How do I know what a public job pays before I apply?

Check the posting first. Many public jobs post a pay range: on November 20, 2026, 70% of the jobs on PublicJobs.ca did. If a posting has no pay, ask before the offer stage.
